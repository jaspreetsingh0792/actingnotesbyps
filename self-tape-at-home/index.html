How to do a self-tape at home: a 10-step checklist

You don’t need a studio. You need a phone, a window and ten minutes of preparation. Most self-tapes are lost on things that have nothing to do with talent: bad light, a wandering eyeline, a performance that was too big for the frame. Here’s how to get all of that right.

Pushpinder SinghActor and acting coach, Gurgaon · 15 October 2026

01Read the brief twice

Before you think about acting, read the casting brief twice and write down exactly what it asks for: the framing, whether they want a slate (your name, height, location) and where it goes, how the file should be named, and the deadline. Casting teams receive hundreds of tapes. The first filter is simply whether you followed instructions.

02Set up your phone properly

  • Horizontal, unless the brief asks for vertical.
  • At your eye level. A stack of books or a cheap tripod works. A camera looking up at you or down at you changes how you read.
  • Wipe the lens. A fingerprint softens the whole image.
  • Lock focus and exposure by pressing and holding on your face in the camera app, so the picture doesn’t pulse while you act.
  • Use the back camera if you can get someone to press record. It’s sharper than the front one.

03Light your face, not the room

The simplest good light is a window in front of you, slightly to one side. Face the window; never put it behind you, or you’ll become a silhouette. Switch off overhead tube lights that create shadows under your eyes. Shoot in the daytime if you can. If you have to shoot at night, put one soft lamp in front of you at face height, bounced off a white wall.

04Fix your sound and background

Casting will forgive average picture. They won’t forgive sound they can’t hear. Record in a small room with soft things in it (curtains, a bed, a cupboard of clothes), close the windows, and switch off the fan and AC for the take. Stand in front of a plain wall, with a metre or so of space behind you so you don’t throw a shadow on it.

05Choose the right frame, and play to its size

Most self-tapes are framed chest up, with a little space above your head. This matters because the same performance that looks natural in a wide shot looks like shouting in a close-up. The tighter the frame, the less your body matters and the more your thoughts do.

Energy is not size. When you’re told to “bring it down”, don’t switch off. Keep the thought at full strength and make only the outward signal smaller.

06Get your eyeline right

Unless the brief says otherwise, don’t look into the lens. Look at your reader’s eye that is nearest the camera, standing just beside it. That keeps both your eyes visible and makes the shot feel intimate. If you have no reader in the room, stick a small piece of tape beside the lens at eye height and play to that. Only move your eyes when a new thought arrives; drifting eyes read as nerves on camera.

07Brief your reader

Your reader stands next to the camera and reads the other lines. Ask them to read flat and a little quieter than you, without acting, so the microphone doesn’t pick them up louder than you. Practise your cues once together. And while they read, actually listen. The camera sees whether you’re listening or just waiting for your line.

08Prepare the thoughts, not just the words

Learn the lines well enough to forget them. Then, for every line, write one sentence of what you’re really thinking underneath it. Choose two or three moments of silence where a thought lands before you speak. On camera, those silences are often the most watched part of the scene.

09Release your face before every take

Tension shows up on camera as a knitted forehead, a clenched jaw and tight lips. Before each take: scrunch your whole face for three seconds and let go, yawn or gently move the jaw side to side, roll your shoulders back and down, then take two slow breaths and drop into your first thought. You want a relaxed face and a busy mind.

10Watch it back twice, then send it early

Watch your best take with the sound off: can you follow what you’re thinking? Then watch it with the sound on: are the key moments there, and does the pace feel alive? Trim the start and end, name the file exactly as the brief asks, and send it before the deadline, not on it.

The quick checklist

  • Brief read twice: framing, slate, file name, deadline
  • Phone horizontal, at eye level, lens clean, focus locked
  • Facing the window, fan and AC off, plain wall behind
  • Framed chest up with a little headroom
  • Eyeline on the reader’s eye nearest the lens
  • Reader briefed: flat and a little quieter than you
  • One-sentence thought written for every line
  • Face release before each take
  • Watched with sound off, then on
  • Trimmed, named correctly, sent early

Common questions

Should I look into the camera in a self-tape?

No, unless the casting brief asks you to. Look at the eye of your reader that is nearest the lens. Looking straight into the lens breaks the scene, and the take usually can’t be used.

Should a self-tape be horizontal or vertical?

Horizontal, unless the brief specifically asks for vertical.

Can I make a good self-tape with just a phone?

Yes. Casting teams care far more about clear sound, soft even light, a steady frame and a truthful performance than about expensive equipment.

How many takes should I do?

Do as many as you need to warm up, but send one take per scene unless the brief asks for more. Pick the take where you can see the thought most clearly, not the one where you worked hardest.
