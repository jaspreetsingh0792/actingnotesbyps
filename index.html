What goes wrong, and where the guides fix it.

Problems actors, coaches and casting people keep describing in public articles and forums, paraphrased with their sources. Next to each is the part of the guide that deals with it.

  1. Reel AGuide 01

    On camera

    Five notes the lens gives actors. Each one is handled in The Camera Actor’s Toolkit.

  2. A1 · The note▶ 14A

    “Too much.” Stage-sized acting reads as overacting in a close-up: a tight jaw, eyebrows that jump on every word, big gestures.

    In the Toolkit

    Frame sizes from wide to extreme close-up, so you know how big to play each shot. Stillness, breath and a 60-second face release to drop the tension before you roll.

    Source · Michelle Danner Acting Studio
  3. A2 · The note▶ 15A

    Eyeline is off. Casting sees actors looking at the wrong spot, darting their eyes or talking straight into the lens.

    In the Toolkit

    A full section on eyeline and blinking for close-ups, plus a self-tape setup chapter.

    Source · StageMilk, after 900+ self-tapes
  4. A3 · The note▶ 16A

    Waiting for your cue instead of listening. Pauses show you thinking about your next line, not about what your partner just said.

    In the Toolkit

    The three layers of a line, the silence test and a method for marking your script, so you know what you’re playing when you aren’t speaking.

    Source · Michelle Danner Acting Studio
  5. A4 · The note▶ 17A

    A self-tape means you direct yourself too. Tapes lose out on framing, bad sound and light, a fumbled opening or an ending that cuts off the moment.

    In the Toolkit

    A self-tape setup, a close-up checklist to run before you hit send, and two scene worksheets to prepare with.

    Source · StageMilk
  6. A5 · The note▶ 18A

    Watching yourself back is uncomfortable, and it can make you so self-conscious that it hurts your work.

    In the Toolkit

    Ten short drills on your own phone, a seven-day plan and a practice log, so getting used to the lens happens in small, private doses.

    Source · Backstage
  7. Reel BGuide 02

    Between jobs

    Four notes from the months between roles. Each one is handled in The Side Hustle Survival Kit.

  8. B1 · The note▶ 22B

    The side job eats the audition. Actors need income that still lets them drop everything for a call time.

    In the Survival Kit

    A three-part test for any side work, and 17 side hustles rated for flexibility and how well they fit with acting.

    Source · BOOM Masterclass
  9. B2 · The note▶ 23B

    Not sure how to start earning. New actors in Mumbai are told to pick up part-time work like content writing, voice-over or events to cover costs while they audition.

    In the Survival Kit

    A plan for your first paid gig in 14 days, with message templates and a formula for the lowest rate you should accept.

    Source · Mumbai audition guide for new actors
  10. B3 · The note▶ 24B

    Payments arrive late, then all at once. CINTAA’s president has called the TV industry’s 90-day payment cycle unfair to actors.

    In the Survival Kit

    Three numbers to know, three accounts to keep, and a plan for what to do the day a big payment finally lands.

    Source · NewKerala, July 2026
  11. B4 · The note▶ 25B

    Work comes and goes, so money is hard to plan and the craft slips during slow months.

    In the Survival Kit

    A working actor’s week, a routine for staying sharp between jobs and a 90-day review to check where you are.

    Source · BOOM Masterclass

INT. Extreme close-up — no dialogue

The Camera Actor’s Toolkit

Dialogue vs. Silence: non-verbal storytelling in extreme close-ups.

What it is. A practical workbook for acting in front of a camera, where the lens sits a few feet from your face and picks up every thought. It starts with how shot sizes change what the audience sees, then works down to the smallest things a close-up shows: your eyes, your blinks, your breath and the moments when you say nothing.

Who it’s for. Actors coming from theatre who keep hearing “smaller”, anyone sending self-tapes for auditions, and creators who want to look natural on screen.

How you use it. Read a chapter, run the matching drill on your phone, log it, then use the checklist and worksheets on your next real scene.

What’s inside

  • Frame sizes from wide to extreme close-up, and why thought is the real performance
  • The three layers of a line, the silence test, marking your script and listening on camera
  • Eyeline, blinking, breath, stillness and a 60-second face release for close-ups
  • Ten phone drills, a seven-day plan and a practice log
  • A self-tape setup, a close-up checklist and two scene worksheets

What’s inside
